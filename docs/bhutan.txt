Bhutan is a small Himalayan kingdom where Buddhism shapes almost every part of daily life. The dominant school is Vajrayana Buddhism, and the Drukpa Kagyu tradition is the state religion. Prayer flags fly from ridges and bridges, prayer wheels line the paths near temples, and monasteries sit on hilltops across the country.

The dzong is the most important type of traditional building in Bhutan. A dzong is a large fortress that serves as both a monastery and the administrative office of a district. Famous examples include Punakha Dzong, Trongsa Dzong, and Tashichho Dzong in the capital, Thimphu. Dzongs are built in a traditional style with thick whitewashed walls, wooden balconies, and richly painted details.

Paro Taktsang, also known as the Tiger's Nest Monastery, is the best-known religious site in Bhutan. It clings to a cliff high above the Paro valley. According to tradition, the Buddhist teacher Guru Rinpoche meditated in a cave at this spot. The monastery complex was built around the cave in 1692, and visitors hike for a few hours to reach it.

Tshechu festivals are held in towns and villages across the year in honor of Guru Rinpoche. During a tshechu, monks and laypeople perform masked dances called cham, wearing colorful costumes. People dress in their finest clothes and travel from far away to attend, since watching the dances is believed to bring blessings.

The national language is Dzongkha, though many other languages are spoken in different regions. English is widely used in schools and government. The national dress is the gho for men, a knee-length robe tied at the waist with a belt, and the kira for women, an ankle-length wrapped dress. Wearing the national dress is expected in schools, government offices, and during formal occasions.

Archery is the national sport of Bhutan. Matches are lively social events with singing, dancing, and teasing between rival teams. Targets are placed more than one hundred meters apart, which makes the sport very challenging.

Bhutanese food is known for its use of chili peppers. The national dish is ema datshi, a stew of chili peppers and cheese, usually eaten with red rice. Another common drink is suja, a salty butter tea served to guests as a sign of hospitality.

Traditional arts are taught through a system called Zorig Chusum, which means the thirteen arts and crafts. These include painting, woodcarving, stone carving, weaving, embroidery, and sculpture. Thangka paintings, which show Buddhist deities and scenes on cloth, are one of the best-known forms of Bhutanese art.
